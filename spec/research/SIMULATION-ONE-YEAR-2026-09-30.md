# One-Year Company Simulation — PaySwap

Date: 2026-09-30
Simulation type: synthetic architecture stress test, not market research or forecast.

## Scenario

Assume PaySwap launches with:
- 3 founders/operators;
- a compact engineering group;
- 36 customer organizations;
- 12 industries;
- 3 small, 3 medium and 3 large firms per industry;
- 300 complex projects per firm during the year;
- 27,180 professionals represented by worker agents across the firms;
- existing industry systems retained as systems of record;
- PaySwap positioned as the main agentic interface and economic orchestration layer.

Total simulated projects: 10,800.

## Competitive reference stacks

Examples used in the simulation:
- construction/engineering: Procore + Autodesk Forma/Bentley;
- finance/accounting: SAP/Oracle/NetSuite/QuickBooks + banking systems;
- sales: Salesforce/HubSpot;
- technology: Jira/Confluence/GitHub/Slack/Teams;
- healthcare: Epic/Oracle Health + Microsoft 365;
- transportation: Samsara + TMS/dispatch tools;
- hospitality: Mews/Oracle Hospitality;
- fashion/media: Shopify + Adobe/CRM/project tools;
- legal: Clio/Relativity + Microsoft 365;
- defense/security: Palantir + ServiceNow/Microsoft ecosystem;
- manufacturing: SAP/Oracle + industrial engineering systems;
- professional services: Salesforce + Microsoft/Google + project-management tools.

These were selected as representative reference stacks, not claims of universal market share.

## Year simulation

### Q1 — discovery and onboarding
The strongest initial signal is that users like a universal agent interface, but agents cannot reliably execute deep work until external data is mapped.

Problems:
- no universal Work Graph;
- connector contracts were too payment-centric;
- permissions had no canonical external-object mapping;
- UI could explain economic actions but not the surrounding project context;
- customers still needed multiple tabs for documents, tasks and industry systems.

Result: high agent interest but low main-interface retention.

### Q2 — connector expansion
PSP connectors work well because PaySwap's underlying capability model is strong.

General enterprise connectors expose the deeper problem:
- "read" is not enough;
- users need search, create, update, approve, attach, reconcile, subscribe and rollback/compensation;
- external IDs and version conflicts become unavoidable;
- provider-specific schemas leak unless aggressively isolated.

The Connector Capability Pack model resolves this.

### Q3 — scale
At hundreds of projects per firm, long-running work becomes dominant.

Problems:
- agents need project context across months, not chat-session memory;
- users need a universal inbox for human/agent exceptions;
- external stakeholders need scoped access;
- offline field workers appear in construction, transportation, hospitality and operations;
- data freshness and conflicting source systems become operational issues;
- enterprise procurement/security begins to block deployment before product capability does.

This produces the Work Operating Plane and Regulatory/Commercial layer.

### Q4 — network effects
Once sufficient connectors and capabilities exist, economic intelligence becomes much more valuable.

Examples:
- detect a project cash-flow gap;
- discover a lender;
- recruit LP liquidity with an incentive program;
- route supplier payments through cheaper rails;
- delay/nets payments;
- initiate service access through a certified smart-contract capability;
- surface an opportunity to switch provider/connector;
- coordinate external experts.

The main remaining bottleneck is depth in specialized systems, not payment capability.

## Synthetic failure/event rates

These are stress-test assumptions generated by the experiment, not observed industry statistics.

Approximate fraction of projects experiencing the issue at least once:
- 78%: missing project/case context;
- 55%: context switching across systems;
- 42%: external data/schema/version mismatch;
- 38%: procurement/security requirements;
- 35%: connector maintenance/provider API change;
- 31%: permission/authorization friction;
- 27%: long-running workflow/handoff problem;
- 22%: offline/field requirement;
- 18%: jurisdiction/regulatory constraint surfaced late;
- 17%: provider outage/reconciliation problem;
- 14%: smart-contract wallet/gas/recovery UX issue;
- 11%: commercial/pricing/revenue-share ambiguity.

The highest-impact architectural failures are not failures of the financial protocol. They are failures to provide universal work context and durable external-system execution.

## Architecture conclusions

1. Add Work Operating Plane.
2. Generalize connectors beyond PSPs.
3. Define Connector Capability Packs.
4. Add external-object identity/version/conflict semantics.
5. Add universal inbox and command center.
6. Add offline/field synchronization.
7. Add enterprise procurement/regulatory perimeter.
8. Separate PaySwap company billing from network financial flows.
9. Track connector/provider SLOs separately from PaySwap SLOs.
10. Treat external systems as systems of record where appropriate instead of attempting a big-bang replacement.

## Product coherence conclusion

PaySwap is coherent when positioned as:
"the agentic operating layer that understands the work, coordinates the systems, and optimizes the economics."

It becomes incoherent if positioned as:
"replace every vertical software product with one giant PaySwap database."

The first is achievable through the architecture. The second creates an unbounded product surface and would recreate each incumbent's deepest domain-specific functionality.
